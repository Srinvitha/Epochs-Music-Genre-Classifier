# Music Theory for Creation: Rhythm, Harmony and Form

Concise original study notes based on concepts covered by Open Music Theory, especially rhythm, pop/rock harmony, and form.

## Rhythm, meter and groove
- **Beat:** a perceived pulse used to organize musical time.
- **Tempo:** the rate of the beat, commonly expressed in beats per minute (BPM).
- **Meter:** recurring grouping of beats, such as groups of two, three or four.
- **Subdivision:** dividing beats into smaller rhythmic units.
- **Syncopation:** emphasizing or articulating places that conflict with expected metrical accents.
- **Groove:** a perceptual result of timing, accents, articulation, repetition and sound; BPM alone does not define it.
- For production, experiment with kick/snare placement, subdivision, note length, velocity and rests. Change one variable at a time when a beat feels stiff.

## Harmony and chord progressions
- A chord progression is a sequence of harmonies. Its effect depends on context, voicing, rhythm, register, melody, timbre and repetition—not chord symbols alone.
- **Tonic** often functions as a point of stability; **dominant** harmony can create directed tension toward resolution in tonal music. These are tendencies, not universal emotional laws.
- Repeating a short progression can establish identity. Variation can come from voicing, bass movement, rhythm, instrumentation or chord-change timing.
- A progression associated with one style can appear in other styles; chord progressions alone do not establish genre.
- For a short clip, a two- or four-chord loop may be enough. Try one clear harmonic idea before adding complexity.

## Form and arrangement
- Musical form describes how sections and ideas are organized over time.
- Repetition helps listeners recognize a motif; contrast helps sections feel different.
- Contrast can come from harmony, register, texture, dynamics, rhythm, instrumentation or silence.
- A short-form cue may use a compressed form: establish a motif, develop or interrupt it, deliver a peak, then resolve or loop.
- A build does not have to become louder; it can add rhythmic density, increase tension, narrow the sound, or gradually reveal a motif.

## Practical creation experiment
1. Choose one emotional intention and a short duration.
2. Make a simple rhythmic or melodic motif.
3. Repeat it enough to establish identity.
4. Change one dimension for the next phrase.
5. Resolve, create a deliberate tail, or lead back into the opening.

## Limits
These concepts provide a vocabulary for composition. They do not let Epochs infer the uploaded song's key, chords, melody or formal sections from current genre probabilities, BPM, RMS energy and duration alone.
