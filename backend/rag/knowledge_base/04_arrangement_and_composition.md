# Arrangement and Composition — Creator Reference

## A useful planning sequence
1. Define the intended emotion and use case.
2. Pick a primary motif: a rhythm, bass figure, chord loop, melodic phrase, or texture.
3. Decide when the listener should first notice it.
4. Create contrast through density, register, rhythm, space, or timbre.
5. Design an ending or loop point deliberately.

## Harmony and melody
- Chords can establish emotional color, but mood is not determined by chord labels alone; rhythm, timbre, register, tempo, and context matter.
- A short motif with a clear rhythmic identity can be more memorable than a complex melody.
- Try changing one dimension at a time: chord voicing, bass note, rhythm, register, or instrumentation.
- Avoid claiming that a progression guarantees a specific emotion.

## Bass and rhythm
- Decide whether bass follows roots, answers the kick, sustains a drone, or provides a counter-rhythm.
- Make kick and bass roles complementary. If both occupy the same rhythmic and spectral space, simplify or change one.
- Groove can come from note placement, duration, velocity, swing, and silence—not just BPM.

## Contrast and development
- Contrast can be created by adding or removing layers, changing register, filtering, changing rhythmic density, or introducing silence.
- A build does not always need to get louder; it can become narrower, faster, more harmonically tense, or more rhythmically dense.
- A drop or reveal is effective when it contrasts with what came before.

## Short-form arrangement templates

### 5–8 seconds: immediate identity
- 0–1s: start with the signature sound or a short pickup.
- 1–5s: state the main motif clearly.
- Final seconds: create a clean stop, impact, or loop point.

### 10–15 seconds: mini narrative
- 0–3s: establish mood or tension.
- 3–7s: introduce pulse or a new layer.
- 7–12s: reveal the main motif or strongest contrast.
- Final 2–3s: resolve, impact, or prepare a loop.
Adapt timestamps to the desired pacing; do not treat them as mandatory structure.

### 20–30 seconds: small arc
- Opening: establish identity.
- Middle: develop or contrast the motif.
- Peak: deliver the strongest moment.
- Ending: resolve, leave a tail, or connect to the opening.

## Loop design
- Match rhythmic phrasing and ambience across the boundary.
- Avoid abrupt changes in reverb tails or background noise unless intentional.
- Try ending on a pickup that leads into the first beat.
- Test the loop repeatedly; visual waveform similarity alone does not guarantee a seamless musical loop.

## Timing and tempo
- BPM is a measured or inferred pulse rate, not a complete description of groove.
- Half-time and double-time interpretations can make the same tempo feel different.
- When suggesting a new BPM, label it as a creative starting point and explain the intended feel.

## Remix arrangement
A short remix can preserve one identity element (motif, chord shape, rhythmic accent, or lyrical phrase if rights allow) while changing two or three other dimensions. Avoid promising preservation of melody, vocals, or stems unless the relevant audio-processing capability exists.
